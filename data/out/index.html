This paper revisits the exchange rate predictability of commodity currencies through the lens of market efficiency. Focusing on currencies with strong contemporaneous exposure to commodity prices, we show that changes in country-specific commodity export or import prices predict exchange rate fluctuations both in sample and out of sample. We establish four empirical facts: (i) the sign of the predictive relationship runs counter to standard risk-based explanations; (ii) predictability is concentrated at short horizons; (iii) predictability is driven by a small set of thinly traded currencies; and (iv) even sophisticated speculative traders adjust positions sluggishly in response to commodity price shocks in these currencies. Together, these findings suggest that predictability arises from the slow incorporation of information in less actively traded currencies, which is likely due to liquidity and position-building constraints, rather than from compensation for risk. The results also clarify the sources of commodity-based predictability in carry trade returns. This paper was accepted by Lukas Schmid, finance. Supplemental Material: The online appendix and data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.07249 .

Two empirical patterns emerge in business-to-business settings, particularly in retail distribution channels such as grocery markets. First, linear contracts are widely observed alongside nonlinear contracts with fixed payments, despite the claimed efficiency advantages of the latter. Second, when fixed payments arise, they often flow from suppliers to retailers (slotting fees) rather than the reverse. We develop and analyze a model of multilateral bargaining in which a supplier negotiates separately with competing retailers to explain these patterns. The key mechanism is a trade-off between two distortions: under linear contracts, wholesale prices reflect bargaining power and can be either above or below the channel-profit-maximizing level, whereas under two-part tariffs, wholesale prices are set to maximize bilateral surplus independently of bargaining power but are systematically too low from an overall channel perspective because of rent shifting across relationships. As a result, neither contractual form uniformly dominates; instead, relative performance depends on how bargaining shapes gains from trade across firms. When bargaining leads to slotting fees, two-part tariffs yield higher channel profits than linear contracts, whereas when it would imply payments from retailers to suppliers, linear contracts may perform better. These findings offer a unified explanation for the persistence of linear contracts alongside two-part tariffs, the direction of fixed payments, and the conditions under which each contractual form is likely to arise. This paper was accepted by Miguel Villas-Boas, marketing. Supplemental Material: The online appendix is available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.03426 .

Abstract Since the 2000s, U.S. public pension funds have actively shifted their risky investments away from public equities and toward alternative assets like private equity and hedge funds—some much more than others. We explore a range of possible explanations for these trends and argue that beliefs about the risk-adjusted return of alternatives have played a central role. Pension beliefs are shaped by investment consultants, peers, and experience in the 1990s. Other demand-side channels related to risk-seeking motives, spending needs, and fund governance have weaker empirical support. We also consider supply-side factors and agency interpretations of the data. (JEL G11, G23, G41, H75)
