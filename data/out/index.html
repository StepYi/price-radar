We examine how funding deficit pressures shape the risk-taking of U.S. corporate defined-benefit pension plans and how targeted regulatory relief can mitigate these incentives. Using the 2012 Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century Act reform, which temporarily eased funding pressure by revising discount rate calculations, we show that plans receiving greater relief significantly reduced their equity allocations and shifted toward safer assets. This derisking was most pronounced among active plans and those sponsored by more financially constrained firms. Our findings demonstrate that targeted regulatory relief can curb risk-taking incentives even without altering underlying economic conditions or financial reporting standards and shed light on the likely (and unlikely) frictions driving such behavior. We quantify the equilibrium impact of retail traders on the U.S. equity market within a structural model of asset demand. Estimating investor-specific demand curves, we find that Robinhood traders are fundamentally distinct from all other investors, including other households: their demand is the most elastic and tilts most strongly toward volatile and microcap stocks. Each dollar invested through Robinhood generates more than five dollars in aggregate equity repricing, exceeding hedge funds, other households, and all other investor types. We decompose the cross-sectional variation in stock returns during Robinhood’s growth surge in 2020 into investor-specific components and find that shifts in Robinhood traders’ latent demand drove 11% of small-cap return volatility despite their negligible aggregate wealth share. To explain these demand shifts, we show that Robinhood traders are the only investor group whose latent demand is well explained by attention proxies: their demand exhibits a V-shaped response to past returns and rises sharply with trading volume. Our findings suggest that the growth of retail trading platforms represents a fundamental shift in the composition of equity demand with equilibrium consequences disproportionate to retail investors’ share of aggregate wealth. This paper was accepted by Lukas Schmid, finance. Funding: P. van der Beck acknowledges financial support from the Swiss National Science Foundation. Supplemental Material: The data files are available at https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.04854 .

ABSTRACT We develop Galerkin–ARIMA, a projection‐based forecasting framework that expands observation‐ and innovation‐lag states with finite basis functions while nesting classical multiplicative SARIMA as a restricted linear specification. The model is estimated using preliminary residual proxies followed by a joint least‐squares or ridge regression, which supports efficient rolling refitting. For the fixed‐dimensional unpenalized estimator, we establish asymptotic normality while accounting explicitly for generated regressors. Across 10 US macroeconomic and financial series, Galerkin–ARIMA has competitive one‐step‐ahead forecast accuracy, with nonlinear gains varying across series and the linear specification providing the most stable performance. In pre‐specified runtime experiments on Real GDP, the S&P 500, and the Unemployment Rate, Ridge Galerkin–ARIMA is substantially faster than likelihood/state‐space SARIMA under fixed, initially tuned, and repeatedly retuned rolling specifications. The results suggest that the main value of the framework is flexible lag‐state modeling and computationally efficient forecasting. Nonlinear enrichment does not produce uniform accuracy gains.
